Product Management Final Project · Briefing · Modules 1–2

StreamLine Spotlight

From a 15,000-title warehouse to a few picks viewers trust

StreamLine has 15,000 titles and flat engagement. Viewers see it as a warehouse of mid-tier content. Spotlight replaces the endless scroll with three picks a night, each labeled with the trusted source that vouches for it and matched to the lens each viewer chose, to win back the viewers who value quality over volume.

We must stop StreamLine's slide into a commoditized, mid-tier content warehouse by fixing the endless, low-trust browsing that leaves viewers scrolling for 20 minutes, falling back on comfort rewatches, or leaving for competitors that hand-pick for them. The argument in five lines

  1. Crisis: doing nothing locks in the "warehouse" label while quality-seekers move to curation-led rivals.
  2. Misery: viewers scroll 20 minutes and give up, rewatch comfort shows, trust friends over the algorithm, or cancel.
  3. Bet: trusted sources, labeled. Critic ratings, editors and viewer favorites build the pool; a two-tap setup picks each viewer's lens; the algorithm matches.
  4. Reach: in the app to retain subscribers, and by weekly email to win back lapsed ones.
  5. Prerequisite: fix watchlist sync and cross-device resume first (Phase 0), or every good pick gets lost between phone and TV.

02 / The problem

Viewers are lost at two different moments

Strategic crisis

If we do nothing for 12 months, StreamLine is locked into the "warehouse of mid-tier content" label. Engagement slides from a plateau into decline as quality-focused viewers move to curation-led rivals, leaving us competing on catalog size and price, a race we lose to bigger libraries.

Moment of misery

After 20 minutes of look-alike tiles, viewers close the app without watching (Priya went back to a DVD), rewatch the same few comfort shows (Aisha), trust friends over the algorithm (Sam), or cancel for a competitor that emails them hand-picked films (Tom).

Why this framing matters: it answers the first objection a stakeholder will raise ("why not just fix the bugs?"). The two problems lose viewers at different moments, so they need different fixes.

Patterns we borrow

  • Double features → editor-paired follow-ups replace the sequel loop
  • Themes → mood picks like "Quiet Sundays" (Beth, UXR-09)
  • Guest spotlights → named critics curate a week
  • Small rotations → fewer choices (UXR-12)
  • Editorial writing → the "why this, why now" note

Where we can't compete

The leaders' catalogs are part of their curation. Ours is described as "mid-tier," so Spotlight shouldn't try to out-Criterion Criterion. Editors pick the best of each kind (a great heist film counts), and a pre-launch audit checks we have enough standout titles.

Sources: competitive research notes (Lifehacker, A Good Movie to Watch, Consumer Reports), summarized, not independently verified.

05 / Strategy

Trusted sources, labeled, matched to you

For discerning viewers who value quality over volume (and the overwhelmed evening viewers who share the same pain), we will replace the 15,000-title scroll with Spotlight: three standout picks a night, each labeled with the trusted source that vouches for it (a top critic, our editors, or viewers like them) and explained ("why this, why now"). A one-tap setup chooses each viewer's lens, so film buffs get critic-backed picks and casual viewers get easy crowd-pleasers. Delivered in the app for subscribers and by weekly email for lapsed ones, because competitors are already winning with hand-picked curation, quality-seeking subscribers like Tom are leaving for it, and every month we wait the "warehouse" perception gets harder to reverse. 06 / Trusted sources

Who viewers trust, and how it feeds the algorithm

Viewers trust a source with a name, consistent taste, reasons, selectivity and independence. Our "Because you watched" row has none of these, so participants trust friends (Sam), a competitor's newsletter (Tom) or a human curator (Marcus) instead. Spotlight borrows trust from those sources instead of asking viewers to trust the algorithm.
